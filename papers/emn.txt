                                                                                   1. Introduction
                                           1.1. Motivation and context. This paper fits in a program initiated by Francis Brown [7],
                                           which aims to use algebraic geometry to explain some of the known irrationality proofs and
                                           perhaps discover new ones. One standard approach to irrationality proofs is as follows: In
                                           order to prove that α ∈ R is irrational, one looks for a sequence of nonzero linear forms
                                                                     λn := an + bn α          (an , bn ∈ Q , n ∈ Z>0 )
                                           of sufficiently fast decay such that
                                                                 λn · (the lowest common denominator of an and bn )
                                           tends to zero as n tends to infinity. If such linear forms can be found, irrationality of α will
                                           follow by an easy argument. A more sophisticated version of this approach due to Nesterenko
                                           gives a line of attack for finding lower bounds on the dimension of the Q-span of a given set
                                           of numbers α1 , . . . , αr . As far as the authors know, all the known irrationality and Q-linear
                                           independence proofs about odd Riemann zeta values (in particular, [1], [4], [20], [2], [24]) are
                                           based on variations of this approach.
                                                When α, or more generally, when α1 , . . . , αr are periods of motives, Brown’s philosophy
                                           proposes using algebraic geometry to produce linear forms in α1 , . . . , αr . This philosophy is
                                             2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. 11G99, 11M06, 14F42.

supported by the evidence that almost all linear forms that appear in the known irrationality
and Q-linear independence proofs involving odd zeta values do indeed arise, although only in
hindsight, from algebraic geometry. See [7, §1] and [12, §1] for more details.
     A difficulty in proving irrationality of odd zeta values is the fact that the linear forms
that one constructs often involve too many unwanted terms. For example, one might get a
linear form in 1 and all the Riemann zeta values ζ(n) where n = 2, . . . , N . Since one already
knows Q-linear independence of the numbers 1 and ζ(2m) ∈ π 2m Q× (m ∈ Z>0 ) thanks to
Lindemann, it becomes extremely difficult to obtain sufficiently strong bounds on the linear
forms and the height of the coefficients to deduce anything nontrivial about odd zeta values.
To rectify this, one wants to obtain linear forms with no unwanted terms.
     On the algebro-geometric side, the above challenge is related to finding a geometric
construction of a motive as small as possible with the desired periods. Again, we refer to
Brown’s paper [7] for more details. Here, the term geometric vaguely means to be built out of
the relative cohomology groups of algebraic varieties using operations such as taking kernels
and cokernels in an abelian category of motives.
     For example, in order to prove irrationality of an odd zeta value ζ(2m + 1) (m ∈ Z>0 )
using the above approach, one needs suitable linear forms in 1 and ζ(2m+1). It is known that
there exists a mixed motive Z2m+1 over Q, unique up to isomorphism, that is an extension of
Q(−(2m + 1)) by Q(0) and has a period matrix of the form

goal is to prove Theorem 1.1.1, and compute the coefficients of 1 and G in the linear forms
(given F and t). The idea is to find differential forms that give rise to elements of the de
Rham realization of C. This is done in §7 (Proposition 7.3.1), leading to a non-constructive
motivic proof of Theorem 1.1.1. In fact, we prove a more intrinsic version of Theorem 1.1.1
(see Theorem 7.2.4). This second version seems at first to be stronger, but turns out to be
equivalent to the version stated earlier, even for the purposes of calculation of the coefficients
of 1 and G (see §7.2, in particular, the comments after Theorem 7.2.4, including Remark
7.2.5). In §8 and §9 we make Theorem 1.1.1 (and Theorem 7.2.4) constructive by calculating
the coefficients of 1 and G. Section 8 handles the logarithmic case (t = 0). The computation
of the coefficient of G in this case amounts to an easy residue calculation (see Proposition
8.1.1). The computation of the coefficient of 1 is considerably more involved. Nonetheless,
we calculate this coefficient in §8.2. The approach is completely explicit. Combining formulas
(8.3), (8.4), and Lemmas 8.2.1 and 8.2.3 one can easily compute the coefficient of 1 in the
linear forms in the logarithmic case. One also obtains bounds on the size of the denominators
(see Theorem 8.3.1). Finally, in §9 we make the case t > 0 of the theorem constructive, by
using an elementary procedure to reduce the order of the pole first.

                           2. The motivic setting and notation
     Let F be a subfield of C. By a variety over F (or simply, a variety, when F is understood
from the context) we mean a quasi-projective reduced scheme of finite type over F . Through-
out the paper, we work in a suitable category MM(F ) of mixed motives over F (specifically,
we shall need F = Q and occasionally F = Q(i)). To be concrete, the reader can take this
to be the Tannakian category of (non-effective) Nori motives over F with rational coefficients
[17], or the category of mixed absolute Hodge motives over F as in [17, §6.3] and [16] (note
that these versions of the theory include motives of non-smooth varieties as well as motives
for relative (co)homology). What we precisely require from MM(F ) is the following:
    (1) MM(F ) is a rigid abelian tensor category in the sense of [11] with End(1) = Q,
        where 1 is the unit object for the tensor product. (Note, importantly, that we work
        with motives with coefficients in Q.)
                                                                                                5

     (2) There is a weight filtration W• on objects of MM(F ). This means that W• is in-
         dexed by Z, increasing, finite on every object, functorial, exact, and compatible with
         the tensor structure. (Functoriality and exactness amount to strict compatibility of
         morphisms with the weight filtration.)
     (3) There exist functorial dual objects H n (X) and Hn (X) in MM(F ) for the (co)homology
         of varieties (over F ). There are also functorial dual objects H n (X, Z) and Hn (X, Z)
         for the relative (co)homology of pairs (X, Z) of a variety X and a closed subvariety
         Z ⊂ X. When the second entry of the pair is the empty subvariety, the relative
         (co)homology recovers the absolute (co)homology. We have H0 (Spec F ) = 1.
     (4) There exist Betti, de Rham and Hodge realization functors with values respectively
         in the categories of vector spaces over Q and F , and the category of rational mixed
         Hodge structures. These functors are exact, faithful, Q-linear, and compatible with
         the tensor structures (in particular, they are conservative). Thus the Betti real-
         ization makes MM(F ) a neutral Tannakian category over Q in the sense of [11].
         The realizations of the (co)homology motives are exactly those expected: the Betti
         realizations of H n (X, Z) and Hn (X, Z) are the (degree n) rational singular coho-
         mology and homology of the analytification of (X, Z); the de Rham realization of
         H n (X, Z) is the relative algebraic de Rham cohomology of (X, Z); and the Hodge
         realization is Deligne’s mixed Hodge structure on the rational singular (co)homology.
         The Hodge realization functor translates the motivic weight to the Hodge-theoretic
         weight. In particular, the Lefschetz motive Q(−1) := H 2 (P1 ) is pure of weight 2 and
         1-dimensional in each of its realizations.
     (5) The following long exact sequences exist in MM(F ):
            - the long exact sequences for the relative cohomology and homology of a pair
              (X, Z) as above, related to each other by duality;
            - the Mayer–Vietoris long exact sequence for an open covering X = U ∪ V of a
              variety X;
            - the Gysin long exact sequence for any triple (X, Z, A) of a smooth variety X,
              a closed subvariety Z ⊂ X, and a smooth closed subvariety A ⊂ X of pure
              codimension.
         The Betti and de Rham realizations of these sequences are the corresponding se-
         quences in the relevant theory.
     (6) There is a distinguished isomorphism called the period isomorphism between the
         complexifications of the Betti and de Rham realization functors, extending the usual
         Betti–de Rham comparison isomorphism for the motives H n (X) of smooth varieties
         to all objects of MM(F ). In the case of the motives of the form H n (X, Z), the
         associated pairing (i.e., the period pairing) between the Betti realization of Hn (X, Z)
         and the de Rham realization of H n (X, Z) is as in [17, §5.3 – §5.5].
     An excellent reference for almost all of the above in the settings of absolute Hodge and
Nori is [17]. In particular, see §3.2 and §3.3 therein for a detailed exposition of the relative
algebraic de Rham cohomology. Existence of the long exact sequences of item (5) in the
setting of Nori motives (which by Proposition 10.1.2 of [17] and its proof also implies the
existence of these exact sequences in the absolute Hodge setting) is probably well-known to
some experts. For a reference, we refer the reader to [14], where the statements are proved in
the more general setting of the authors’ exponential motives (see op. cit., §4.2.4 for the relative
(co)homology sequence, Corollary 4.6.5 for the Mayer–Vietoris sequence, and Theorem 4.8.3
for the Gysin sequence). By §5.1 of [14], taking the potential function to be zero, one recovers
the desired statements for Nori motives from those for exponential motives.

9.1. Overview. The goal of this section is to make Theorem 7.2.4 constructive in the case
t > 0, that is, when our differential form
                                                   F dxdy
                                          ωF,t =
                                                    g t+1
may have a non-simple pole along A. The motivation for this is the hope that it may be easier
to find better-behaved linear forms by allowing the order of the pole to grow.
     In §9.2-§9.4 below we handle the computation of the coefficients of 1 and G in the
linear form associated with ωF,t under the hypotheses of Theorem 1.1.1, i.e., assuming (i)
F ∈ Q[x, y]σ and (ii) if t is even (resp. odd), xt y t (resp. xt+1 y t+1 ) divides F . Our approach
to compute the coefficients of 1 and G under these hypotheses is to reduce the order of the pole
to 1, the case for which we already know how to compute the coefficients. The process for the
reduction of the order of the pole is elementary, but we will need to be careful about preserving
σ-invariance and regularity in the process. Condition (ii) of Theorem 1.1.1 will allow us to
maintain nice regularity properties throughout the process. The pole order reduction process
will be explicit, in that combined with the explicit calculations of the coefficients of 1 and G
in the simple pole case, we can explicitly compute the coefficients of 1 and G in the linear
form coming from every F and t as in Theorem 1.1.1.
     Finally, in §9.5 we will see that the computation of the coefficients of 1 and G in the
linear form associated with an arbitrary ωF,t as in Theorem 7.2.4 can be reduced to the case
considered in Theorem 1.1.1.

Remark 9.5.2. For the sake of completeness, here we record another version of Theorem 7.2.4:
for every t ∈ Z≥0 and F ∈ Q[x2 , y 2 ], if ωF,t is integrable, then the integral of ωF,t over ∆ is
in ⟨1, G⟩Q . The equivalence of this statement with Theorem 7.2.4 follows from the following
three facts: (i) For any F ∈ Q[x, y] with integrable ωF,t , writing F = F+ + F− according to
the decomposition (7.6), both ωF+ ,t and ωF− ,t are integrable; (ii) the integral of ωF− ,t over
∆ is zero; and (iii) both Q[x2 , y 2 ] and Q[x, y]σ contain Q[x, y]σ,τ , and the images of Q[x2 , y 2 ]
and Q[x, y]σ under the eigenspace projection map Q[x, y] → Q[x, y]τ are both Q[x, y]σ,τ .
